Five signs your business is ready for AI, and three signs it isn’t

By Atam Singh · November 25, 2026 · 5 min read

Most businesses are ready for one small, well-chosen AI project sooner than they think, and some should wait. This self-check gives you a clear answer either way, and “not yet” is a perfectly good one.

What “not relevant” often means

Statistics Canada’s Canadian Survey on Business Conditions for the third quarter of 2026 found that 25.2% of Canadian businesses plan to use AI in the next 12 months. A year earlier the figure was 14.5%, and the year before that it was 10.6%.

The other half of the survey interests me more. Just over half of businesses, 52.7%, have no plans at all. Of those, 79.1% say AI is not relevant to what they do.

For some of them that is simply true. But when I hear “not relevant” from a business owner, it often means “nobody has shown me where it fits”. A survey cannot tell those two answers apart. The checklist below can.

Five signs you are ready

Each sign comes with a one-line test. Apply the tests to one specific task, not to the business as a whole.

1. A repetitive task eats several hours every week

Think of the Monday report, quotes built from the same price list, or invoices keyed from emails into the accounting package. The work follows a pattern, and a capable person does most of it on autopilot.

The test: write the steps on one page. If a new hire could follow them, the task is a candidate. I walk through a common example in the weekly report that builds itself.

2. The information already exists in digital form

AI works on what it can read: spreadsheets, emails, PDFs, records in your CRM (the system where you keep customer details). The information does not have to be perfect, but it has to live somewhere a computer can reach.

The test: open every file or system the task depends on, today, without asking anyone to remember something. If you can, you pass.

3. Someone owns the process and wants it fixed

The projects I have seen work had one named person who knew the task, felt the pain and had the authority to change how it was done. Without that person, the tool gets built and then quietly ignored.

The test: write down one name. If you wrote a department, or “everyone”, you do not have an owner yet.

4. You can say what “better” looks like in a number

Hours per week, errors per month, time to answer a customer. One number is enough. It tells you what to build, and later it tells you whether the money was well spent.

The test: finish this sentence: “Today this takes X, and I would be happy with Y.” A rough estimate is fine. A small example from my own work: automating the preparation for a release that happened every two weeks gave the team back two hours each time. Not dramatic, but it was a number we could check.

5. You are willing to start with one small project

A company-wide rollout spreads attention across ten things and proves none of them. One small project gives you a result you can judge and a team that has seen it work.

Inside a federal department, the first dashboard I built replaced a single weekly funding report that had been printed and mailed. One report, one audience. That is the size of first project I mean.

The test: describe the project in one sentence that names one task and one team.

Three signs you are not ready yet

None of these is a permanent no. Each has a fix, and the fix rarely involves AI.

1. The process is not agreed, or changes every week

If three people prepare a quote three different ways, automating it will only produce the disagreement faster. AI can speed up a settled process. It cannot settle one for you.

What to do: fix the process first. Sit down with the people who do the work, agree on one way, write it down and run it by hand until it stops changing.

2. The information is on paper or in people’s heads

If the honest answer to “where is that written down” is “ask whoever has been here longest”, there is nothing for a tool to work with. It is a common reason projects stall. Gartner predicts that through 2026 organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects that are not supported by AI-ready data, meaning data complete and organized enough for the job.

What to do: start capturing it. Move the paper form into a simple spreadsheet or online form, and ask your most experienced person to write down the rules they apply. That work pays off whether or not you ever use AI. I explain why in why most AI projects fail, and the boring fix.

3. You want AI to solve a problem you have not defined

“We should be doing something with AI” is a worry, not a project. Without a defined problem you cannot choose a tool, set a budget or know when you are finished.

What to do: go back to sign four. Pick the task that frustrates your team most and put a number on it. If you cannot find one, AI may truly not be relevant to you right now, and that is a legitimate answer.

Why “not yet” is a good result

If you landed on “not yet”, the check has done its job. You have avoided paying for a tool that would have sat on top of an unsettled process or missing information, and you know what to fix first.

Sometimes AI is the wrong tool altogether. If a task follows fixed rules and needs no judgment, a spreadsheet formula or a feature already in your accounting package may do it for less. I would sooner tell you that at the start than after you have paid for something.

What to do this week

  1. List three tasks that take your team several hours every week and follow a pattern.
  2. Run the five tests on each one and note which tests it passes.
  3. Pick the task with the most passes. Write one name and one number beside it.
  4. If any of the three “not yet” signs apply to that task, do the fix for that sign before anything else.
