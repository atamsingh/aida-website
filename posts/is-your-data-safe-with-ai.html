Worry about privacy and security is a sensible reason to be careful with AI, but it does not have to be a reason to avoid it. A short list of plain questions, asked before any business information goes in, will tell you most of what you need to know.

The worry is reasonable

Statistics Canada’s Canadian Survey on Business Conditions for the third quarter of 2026 found that 52.7% of Canadian businesses have no plans to use AI in the next 12 months. Of those businesses, 10.8% cite privacy or security concerns.

I think that concern is healthy. Your customer list, your quotes, your invoices and your staff files are worth protecting, and an AI tool is one more place where they can end up.

The trouble starts when the concern stays vague. “Is it safe?” has no useful answer. “Where is our data stored, and who can see it?” does. The questions below are the ones I would put to any AI tool vendor or consultant, myself included.

Seven questions to ask before any business information goes in

Ask for the answers in writing. You do not need to be technical to judge them. A good answer is short, specific and matches what the contract says.

  1. Where is our data stored and processed, and in which country? Some of your customers and contracts may care where their information sits, and your adviser will ask. “In the cloud” is not an answer. A country is.
  2. Is what we type in used to train the AI model for other customers? Training means the vendor uses your text to improve the model that everyone shares. You want a clear yes or no, and to know whether you can switch it off.
  3. Who at the vendor can see our data? Support staff and engineers sometimes need access to fix problems. Ask who, in what circumstances, and whether that access is recorded.
  4. Who inside our business can see what through the tool? If the payroll folder is limited to two people on your shared drive, the tool should not summarize it for everyone else. This matters most with a chatbot that answers from your own documents, which should respect the permissions you already have.
  5. How long is data kept, and can we delete it? Information you cannot delete is information you stay responsible for. Ask for the retention period and how a deletion request works in practice.
  6. What happens to our data if we stop using the service? You should be able to take your information out in a usable form and have the vendor’s copies removed. Ask before you sign, while the vendor still wants your business.
  7. Is there a record of what the AI was asked and what it answered? When an answer turns out to be wrong, a log shows what was asked, what came back and who asked it. Without one, you are guessing.

If a vendor cannot or will not answer these, treat that as your answer. That tool is the wrong one for anything sensitive, however good the demonstration looked.

Privacy is cheaper to design in than to add later

On one project, I led an effort to bring 5 petabytes of unstructured data into privacy compliance. A petabyte is a million gigabytes, and unstructured means files, documents and logs, not tidy tables. On other work, I built data systems under Canada and US data-residency restrictions, meaning limits on which country certain data could be stored and processed in. Rules like that are manageable when you know about them before you build. They are painful when you find out afterwards.

The lesson I took from both is that privacy is far cheaper to design in at the start than to retrofit. The same holds at a much smaller scale. Asking seven questions before you choose a tool takes a few emails. Working out where customer information went after a year of casual use takes a great deal more.

Three habits that lower the risk straight away

You can adopt these before you have chosen any tool at all.

  • Keep customer personal information and confidential documents out of free consumer AI tools. When a staff member signs up for a free tool on their own, your business usually has no contract with the provider, no administrator controls and no answers to the seven questions. Until you have those answers, keep names, contact details, signed contracts and financial records out of it.
  • Start with low-risk information. Begin with material that is already public or would do no harm if it were: product descriptions, a job posting, a how-to guide, the wording of a generic email. Learn what the tool is good and bad at before anything sensitive goes near it.
  • Write a one-page staff guideline. Some of your staff may already be trying these tools. Tell them which tools are approved, what must never be pasted in, and who to ask when they are unsure. One page gets read. A twenty-page policy does not.

Where the law comes in

This post is not legal advice. Canada has a federal privacy law for private-sector businesses, known as PIPEDA, and some provinces, such as Quebec, have their own laws.

I am not going to tell you what those laws require of your business, because that depends on details I cannot see from here. Confirm your obligations with your own legal or privacy adviser. The vendor’s written answers to the seven questions are what that adviser will want to read, so bring them with you.

What to do this week

  1. Ask around and list the AI tools people in your business already use, including free ones, and what kind of information they put into them.
  2. Send the seven questions to the vendor of any AI tool you use or are considering, and ask for written answers.
  3. Write the one-page guideline: approved tools, what never goes in, who to ask. Share it at your next staff meeting.
  4. Pick one low-risk task to try, using information that is already public.
  5. Book time with your legal or privacy adviser and take the vendor’s answers along.
